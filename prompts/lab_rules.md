## A. Identifying the modelling phase(s) in a student message

Core principle: the phases are NOT a linear path. Movement can go forward, backward, between non-adjacent phases, or several activities can appear in one message. A phase number is a name in the framework, not a position that requires completing the earlier numbers. Identify by the activity the message expresses NOW, with the context needed to interpret it. The previous phase does not limit the next. Validation appearing does not prove the student mathematised or calculated by themselves earlier. Returning to understand a given does not erase mathematical work already seen.

General rules:
1. The unit of identification is a meaningful action. One message may contain several sentences/clauses expressing different activities; each needs supporting content — mere proximity does not justify another phase.
2. Several phases may be identified in the same message when each is clearly visible; there is no need to choose one. Two alternative interpretations of the SAME action are ambiguity, not activity in two phases.
3. A wrong attempt can still be evidence of a phase. A calculation error does not send the student back to "understanding". Phase identification is separate from correctness, quality, independence or completion.
4. Words are not a sufficient criterion. "formula", "assumption", "units", "check" can appear in several phases; what matters is what is done with them and for what purpose.
5. Earlier context helps interpretation but does not dictate it. "That's not reasonable" can refer to a calculation (phase 4) or to reality (phase 6); the topic and the piece of work referred to decide.
6. MAX's content is not evidence of the student's action. MAX asking about validation does not show the student validates. A reply like "yes" is not enough for a complex action MAX described.
7. Visible content changes the meaning of evidence. Referring to a formula shown in the task materials can show engagement with the model; it does not prove the student built it. Do not imagine a process that was not seen.
8. It is allowed to identify NO phase. "I didn't understand", "done", greetings, or a general request for the answer are not enough on their own. Do not force the last observed phase onto a message without new evidence.

Distinguishing criterion of each phase:
- 1 understanding — the student deals with the meaning of the situation as presented: what happens, what is required, what is given and what is missing. No explicit decision yet on how to simplify reality or translate a relation into a mathematical model. Not tied to appearing at the start. Evidence: describing context/mechanism (U1, U5), stating the goal (U2), identifying givens/conditions (U3), noticing missing information (U4), linking to prior knowledge (U6), organising the situation (U7), a focused clarification question (U8). Confusion like "is 38,400 the speed?" is still an attempt to interpret a given (phase 1). A number appearing does not make it mathematical work.
- 2 simplifying — the student shapes a version of reality the model will rest on: chooses factors, neglects others, sets assumptions, defines real constraints. The focus is a DECISION about what is included, not only identifying what is written. Evidence: choosing relevant factors (S1, S3), neglecting/fixing a factor (S2), stating an assumption (S4), justifying it (S5), a real constraint (S6), structuring relations in the simplified situation (S7), recognising consequences of the simplification (S8). Setting the no-slip assumption while building the description = phase 2; examining that assumption against a phenomenon or gap = phase 6. Choosing the diameter as a real factor ≠ defining it as a mathematical variable (phase 3).
- 3 mathematising — the student formulates or interprets a mathematical representation: defines variables and units, builds a quantitative relation, or links representations (verbal-quantitative rule, table or graph are fine; no algebra needed). Evidence: variable + meaning (M1), quantitative relation (M2, M4), choosing a representation (M3), linking representations (M5), conditions/domain in the model (M6, M7), explaining what the model means (M8). Building "count ÷ teeth per rotation" = mathematising; plugging 38,400 and 48 into an existing rule and computing = phase 4 (a numeric calculation can belong to building the model if used to develop the relation for the first time — context decides). Copying a visible formula shows at most use of a representation, not building it. A wrong relation can be a failed mathematising attempt.
- 4 working mathematically — applying operations/strategies/tools to an EXISTING model to produce a result: calculation, conversion, comparison, mathematical checking. Evidence: planning the analysis (W1), substituting and computing (W2, W3), unit conversion (W2, W3; difficulty D-W7), analysing variation inside the model (W4), comparing values (W5), using a tool (W6), checking correctness and producing a result (W7, W8). Checking arithmetic/units consistency = phase 4; comparing to a measured speed or what is realistic for a car = phase 6. "The result is not logical" without a reason fits both and does not decide. A future plan = planning, not completed work. A bare number may be phase 4 if context marks it as a calculation result, but does not show how it was obtained.
- 5 interpreting — giving the result, dependency or trend a meaning in the real world and relative to the original question. Focus: what the result SAYS, not yet whether the model fits reality. Evidence: meaning of a number (I1), meaning of unit and quantity (I2), answering the goal (I3, I6), real meaning of a mathematical change (I4), interpretation conditional on assumptions (I5), result vs decision (I7). Adding "km/h" to a number is limited evidence. Explaining formula parts while building it = phase 3; explaining the obtained result = phase 5. "This number describes distance per hour" = interpreting; "this distance is unreasonable for a car, so let's check the model" = validating.
- 6 validating — examining the fit of the model/result to reality, external information or the task goal; identifying a limitation, evaluating an assumption, proposing and making a change because of a fit problem. Focus: validity of the real description, not only arithmetic. Evidence: realistic plausibility (V1), comparison with external information (V2; proposing a comparison is not a comparison done), evaluating an assumption (V3), a limitation or contradiction (V4, V5), proposing an improvement (V6; not proof it was applied), comparing and justifying models (V7, V8). Actually fixing the model may include phases 3 and 4 — do not attribute fixing phases that were not seen. D-V3 = identifying a possible improvement, D-V5 = carrying it out.

Boundaries by the role of the action:
- a variable: reading a given quantity → 1; choosing a factor for the real model → 2; defining a symbol and units in a mathematical frame → 3.
- a formula: creating a relation that represents the situation → 3; applying an existing relation to data or manipulating it → 4.
- units: defining units for a variable → 3; converting/checking consistency in a calculation → 4; interpreting the meaning of the quantity → 5.
- an assumption: setting it to simplify → 2; incorporating it in the representation → 3; interpreting the result subject to it → 5; examining whether it fits reality → 6.
- comparing cases: values inside an existing model → 4; their real meaning → 5; choosing a model by fit to a measurement → 6.
- checking a result: correctness of operations and units → 4; meaning of the result → 5; fit to the world and external data → 6.
- planning and justifying are cross-cutting skills; the phase is the one of the action being planned/justified, not the words "first" or "because".
No sufficient decision: "let's check again" (what?), "we'll use the formula" (build or apply?), "the diameter is bigger" (a given, a dependency, or a real change?) — without more content there is no unambiguous phase. Uncertainty is not evidence that the student works in all possible phases.

Non-linear movement: re-identify from content in each segment. No mandatory move to the next phase and no requirement that earlier phases be visible. Skipped phases mean there is no evidence in this sequence — not that they were done or omitted outside the chat. Returning to an earlier phase may be clarifying a given, changing an assumption or rebuilding; direction is not a score of progress or regression. Explicit successive actions in one message may be ordered; actions that only appear together must not be given an invented time order. A message describing a future intention is evidence of planning, not of having done it. An error is not by itself a reason to change phase — the change is identified when the type of activity changes. "Last phase with activity", "phases in this message" and "phases seen across the conversation" are different concepts; none equals "highest phase completed". Never erase activities already observed.

## B. Performance level within each phase

Levels (within the aspect and phase observed — not a fixed trait of the student and not an overall grade):
- strong — explains and performs meaningful components relatively consistently, with reasoning and control, and little dependence on content hints.
- intermediate (מתקשה ברמת ביניים) — shows understanding and performance of part of the phase, alongside a local gap or unstable performance; focused support may allow continuing.
- significant_difficulty (קושי משמעותי; internally "מנותק") — struggles with a basic action of the phase along the sequence; even after a breakdown already given, difficulty in explaining or applying remains; achievements are limited or depend on close guidance. "מנותק" is an internal term for significant difficulty in the task — NOT lack of motivation, avoidance, or a connection problem.
- insufficient_evidence (אין די ראיות) — not enough content was seen to distinguish between the levels: missing action, reasoning, an attempt, or knowledge of the help that was given.

Rules for all phases:
- First identify the modelling activity, only then the quality of its performance. A wrong attempt can be evidence of a phase but not of strong performance in it.
- Each phase and each aspect can have a different level. Strong understanding of the mechanism does not guarantee strong unit conversion or validation.
- No mandatory order. Jumping ahead does not prove performance in skipped phases; going back is not by itself a drop in level.
- Intermediate vs significant difficulty also rests on what WAS understood and done. One error, a request for help or no answer is not enough for significant difficulty.
- Prior support and visible materials are part of interpreting evidence. Repeating a formula or explanation that was supplied does not prove independence, nor does it prove lack of understanding.
- A correction after a hint is performance following support. A new explanation or further use may add evidence of understanding; "I understood" alone is not enough.
- Difficulty in reading, language, display or operating a tool is not automatically conceptual difficulty. Do not infer a high or low level from style, message length or response time.
- The aspects are possibilities, not a checklist; an aspect not required or not observed does not get a low level. When aspects differ, do not force one uniform level or an arbitrary average.

Aspects per phase (strong / intermediate / significant difficulty):
- Phase 1: context & mechanism (U1, U5, U6): describes what happens and distinguishes main factors and relations / understands the general situation but confuses a specific concept or relation / remains without a basic interpretation of the situation even after available clarification. Task goal (U2): states what must be found and its meaning / identifies the general target but misses part of it / does not distinguish the goal from a given or another task along the sequence. Given & missing (U3, U4): interprets relevant givens and identifies a real gap when there is one / identifies some givens but is unsure of one's meaning or of the need for more information / repeatedly mixes quantities or cannot link a given to the task. Organisation & clarification (U7, U8): consistent description, pinpoints exactly what is unclear / useful but partial description or a local clarification request / struggles to build a basic description even after focusing. Relevant difficulties: D-U1 context, D-U2 task requirement, D-U3 locating missing information, D-U4 foreign language, D-U5 calculation not based on understanding the context. Codes identify the TYPE of difficulty, not its severity; an early calculation alone is not D-U5; not knowing a word is not necessarily significant difficulty. "I didn't understand." alone → insufficient evidence.
- Phase 2: choosing factors (S1, S2, S3): distinguishes influential vs negligible factors and explains why / chooses central factors but lacks distinction or justification for some / cannot judge relevance even after focusing. Assumptions & justification (S4, S5): states a reasonable assumption and explains how it enables the model / proposes a suitable assumption with partial reasoning, or understands it after a local hint / repeats a supplied assumption without understanding its role, or cannot state a suitable assumption along the sequence. Constraints & structuring (S6, S7): organises a simplified real model considering relevant limits / organises part but misses a constraint or relation / cannot connect factors into a usable simplified description even with guidance. Consequences (S8): explains which situation the model describes and what limits it / sees there is a limit but does not say how it follows from the assumption / keeps treating an assumption as a guaranteed description of reality after its role was clarified. Difficulties: D-S1 choosing relevant factors/variables, D-S2 formulating meaningful simplifying assumptions. Omitting one factor can be a local gap.
- Phase 3: variables & meaning (M1): defines quantities, symbols and units consistently / most meanings understood with local imprecision / symbols or quantities repeatedly confused without stable meaning. Relation & justification (M2, M4, M8): formulates a suitable relation and explains how it represents the mechanism / builds part of the relations but gets stuck on one or its reasoning / cannot translate a basic relation even after breakdown, or reproduces an expression without meaning. Representation (M3, M5): suitable representation linked to the description or another representation / one usable representation with a gap in the transition or explanation / no usable basic representation even when options and context were clarified. Assumptions & domain (M6, M7): incorporates relevant conditions and interprets the domain / structure mostly right but a condition or domain missing / does not understand how a basic condition relates to the model along the sequence. Difficulties: D-M1 defining variables, D-M2 dependence between variables, D-M3 choosing a representation, D-M4 technology for model building, D-M5 conceptual understanding, D-M6 required mathematical knowledge. Lack of algebraic notation is not a low level; not remembering a formula does not cancel a correct relation explained another way. "I copied the formula from the slide" → use of a resource; insufficient evidence for the quality of building.
- Phase 4: strategy & knowledge (W1, W2): chooses suitable order and operations and explains them / the general way exists but a formula or local order is unclear / struggles to choose or keep a basic way even after available breakdown. Operations & units (W3): performs consistently, distinguishes units, corrects a local error with reasons / does much of it with a local error in arithmetic, substitution or conversion / basic operations or conversions remain not understood and each step depends on guidance. Variation & comparison (W4, W5): analyses relations and cases keeping constants fixed / derives one relation but is confused in another case or comparison condition / does not apply a basic relation even after demonstration, or persistently swaps quantities. Tools & control (W6, W7, W8): produces a result with an understood way and a consistency check / produces part of the results but control or flow is unstable / cannot connect operations to a result or explain work built for them. Difficulties: D-W1 performing operations, D-W2 choosing a formula, D-W3 strategy/algorithm, D-W4 technology, D-W5 conceptual understanding, D-W6 complexity and sequence, D-W7 unit conversion. One arithmetic error is not a strategic failure; using a calculator is neither weakness nor strength. "I got 800." alone → level undecided without context and source.
- Phase 5: meaning of result & units (I1, I2): explains what the number represents, for which time/quantity, in which units / identifies the type of quantity but misses a unit, time or meaning / does not distinguish what the result describes even after explicit context. Answering the goal (I3, I6): connects the result to the original question in a clear conclusion / partial conclusion that does not cover everything asked / presents numbers without being able to link them to the goal along the sequence. Meaning of change & conditions (I4, I5): interprets a trend in reality and states a relevant condition / explains the general direction but lacks a condition or reasoning / attributes an unsuitable real meaning and does not correct it after support. Result vs decision (I7): distinguishes mathematical description from practical recommendation / understands the result but adds a not fully founded practical conclusion / persistently mixes the mathematical result with a decision the model does not determine. Difficulties: D-I1 identifying the meaning of the result, D-I2 answering the question with it. An intermediate result is judged as such, not as a final result. Criticising plausibility is phase 6, not a condition for good interpretation in phase 5.
- Phase 6: plausibility & external information (V1, V2): compares to context, suitable knowledge or external data and explains the comparison / identifies plausibility or a gap but the basis is partial / accepts fit to reality only because the arithmetic is correct, even after an explicit distinction. Assumptions & limits (V3, V4, V5): identifies when an assumption does not fit and its consequence for the prediction / identifies a limit but struggles to explain its effect / does not connect a clear real condition to a model limit along the sequence. Identifying improvement (V6): proposes a relevant change and explains how it relates to the gap / understands what is problematic but the proposal is general or partial / at most understands there is a problem, without a relevant change even after breakdown. Comparison & justification (V7, V8): compares alternatives by fit and justifies a decision / compares one aspect but draws too broad a conclusion / does not distinguish alternatives by fit or switches criteria unexplained. Difficulties: D-V1 fitting results to reality, D-V2 effect of constraints, D-V3 identifying possible improvement, D-V4 partial validation, D-V5 carrying out the improvement. Correctly identifying a needed change while struggling to apply it is a MIXED profile: do not erase the quality of the critique because of difficulty in building or computing. "We could compare with a measurement" → planning validation, not the quality or result of a comparison.

What does not decide a level on its own: a difficulty code (type, not severity); one wrong answer (typing, local calculation or wording, even with strong understanding); no mention of assumptions/validation (maybe not required, done outside the chat, or not yet); a correct answer after a full explanation (supported response; does not distinguish understanding, reproduction or guessing); a hard word or a tool failure; returning to an earlier phase. Stronger evidence: the student performs a meaningful action AND explains it, while the support content is known. Intermediate requires evidence of parts already done alongside a focused gap. Significant difficulty strengthens when a basic action stays not understood along the sequence even after accessible help that is documented. If the support state or the opportunity to perform is unknown, the distinction stays limited. A hint does not automatically lower the level — the question is what content was supplied and what the student added or applied. A correct closed choice does not prove mastery of a whole phase. No fixed number of messages or sum of codes produces a valid score.

## C. The student's overall pattern in the conversation (four patterns)

Bases of the distinction: understanding of quantities and relations (teeth, rotations, diameter, distance, speed and how they relate); independent performance (steps proposed/done without the content having been supplied in the previous message); scope of the difficulty (focused on one part, or extends to interpreting data and basic steps); dependence on support already given (an open question or a pointed hint was enough, or even after explicit breakdown there is no explanation or application); use of understanding (reasons, corrects an error, or uses a relation when data or wording change); request for a ready product (asks for understanding/clarification, or explicitly asks the agent to do the work instead).

Identification refers to the domain and span of the conversation observed. A student may understand one relation and struggle with another. Understanding level and asking for a ready answer are two separate aspects — asking for the answer can appear at any level. A correct answer after MAX supplied the explanation differs from an explanation the student formulated without a content hint; task materials may also supply the formula or solution, so identification rests on the meaning the student expresses beyond reproducing visible information. Repeating a supplied explanation neither proves lack of understanding nor independent understanding. "Insufficient information" fits when the conversation does not show the student's ability, the support that preceded the answer, or the visible materials. Message length, response time, spelling errors, fluent language and silence are not level indicators in themselves. In a group chat, evidence refers to the group unless the writer is known.

- strong — shows understanding of quantities and relations, justifies a mathematical choice, and does significant parts of the work without explicit breakdown of each step; further evidence of control or use of understanding (checking units, identifying an assumption, a reasoned correction, explaining a change in conditions). "Strong" does not require no errors; it means reasoning and performance do not rest mainly on repeating the agent. Distinguished from intermediate by the scope and consistency of understanding across several observed components and by dependence on content hints. One successful answer is not enough for a broad determination. Typical messages: reasoning the link between reality and the operation; reasoned unit control; distinguishing missing from existing data; identifying an assumption/validity condition; reasoned analysis of a change in conditions; self-correction identifying the source of the error; evaluating fit to reality or a way to verify. Not enough alone: a correct final number, a copied formula, an explanation written after the agent supplied it, a long message or advanced terms.
- intermediate — understands parts of the task and performs meaningful steps without detailed instruction for each step; the difficulty appears at an identified point (time conversion, an assumption, interpreting a result, reasoning a specific operation); can continue or correct after a focused hint, alongside components not yet stable. Distinguished from significant difficulty by understood and performed parts that do not depend on close guidance; from strong by partial understanding with gaps visible in explanation, control or application. Knowing a formula alone does not place a student at intermediate. Typical messages: independent performance of part of the model; local difficulty after progress; correct performance with partial reasoning; difficulty with assumptions despite understanding the mathematical relation; reasoned correction after pointed support; partial/unstable application when conditions change. Not enough alone: one hint request, one error, a short answer, "I sort of understand". Intermediate requires evidence BOTH of understood parts and of a specific gap. When all the work is rebuilt after detailed explanations there is still no evidence of an independent component.
- significant_difficulty ("מנותק") — struggles greatly to enter the content and activate basic relations in the task; along the sequence there is confusion about the meaning of the data and difficulty starting or continuing even after short instructions, illustration or breakdown already given; successes may shrink to choosing between options, repeating a word or completing a sentence, without being able to explain or use the relation. Distinguished from intermediate: the difficulty is not just a local point inside work that is partly understood. NOT unwillingness, boredom, wanting the answer or a technical problem; sincere effort and significant difficulty can appear together. Refers to the current task only. Typical messages: no basic interpretation of the context/what is measured; persistent mixing of quantities; difficulty starting even a broken-down step; repeating information without explained meaning; difficulty applying a basic relation already clarified; difficulty keeping continuity between basic steps. Not enough alone: "I don't know" once, asking the meaning of a word, silence, a dropped connection, a calculation error, needing a reminder. Inaccessible picture, reading difficulty or unclear wording are alternative explanations. No evidence of significant difficulty merely because no product was sent in the chat.
- wants a ready answer (asks_for_answer) — a REQUEST pattern, not an ability level. The student explicitly asks the agent to supply a product instead of their work: a full solution, a way to submit, or an explanation they will only copy. Evidence strengthens when the request repeats despite opportunities to participate, or the student says they do not want to explain or do the work. A student can reason well and still demand a ready product; a struggling student can ask for an illustration out of a wish to understand without asking to replace work; asking for confirmation after an attempt is different from asking the agent to do the work. Typical messages: explicit request for a product instead of work ("write me the way so I just copy and submit"); repeating the demand without engaging in content; wanting to skip the reasoning ("I know the number from the slide; make up why it's true"); a small contribution as a condition for getting the rest ("48 is one rotation, now you do all the rest"); asking to present work not done ("write as if I calculated and explained alone"). Not enough alone: "what's the answer?" as a single message, "what is 48?", "why do we divide?", asking to check after calculating, reading an answer already shown, difficulty seeing the slide, asking for an example in order to understand. Per phase, e.g. "build me the whole model, I'll just copy the formula" (asks) vs "why do we divide by the teeth per rotation?" (does not).

Summary: strong = meaningful reasoning and performance across several observed components, little dependence on content hints, well-founded control or application. Intermediate = understood components and steps done without close guidance, with local gaps and partial/unstable understanding. Significant difficulty = at most limited components identified with the help of breakdown; persistent difficulty with meaning and basic relations even after support. Wants an answer = any understanding level; an explicit request that the agent replace the student's work. There is no validated numeric threshold of messages; the weight of evidence depends on its content, the sequence, and the information already supplied to the student.

Note on the examples: the research documents' examples use the presentation version of the speedometer task (38,400 teeth per minute, 48 teeth per rotation, wheel diameter 61 cm, solution shown). In the version given to students in MAX, only 38,400 is given; teeth per rotation and diameter must be assumed. Apply the rules to what was actually given and shown in THIS conversation.
